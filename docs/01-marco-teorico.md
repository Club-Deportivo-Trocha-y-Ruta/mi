# Theoretical-methodological framework for training youth XCO cyclists (ages 10–15)

**Club Deportivo Trocha y Ruta — Reference document for the coach**

Training mountain bike XCO cyclists between the ages of 10 and 15 requires a radically different approach from that used with adult athletes: the absolute priority is the long-term integral development of the young athlete, not immediate competitive performance. This means that fun, multi-sport participation, technical progression, and the protection of physical and psychological health must govern all planning decisions. Istvan Balyi's Long-Term Athlete Development (LTAD) model classifies cycling as a **late-specialization sport**, which means that specialization before age 15–16 is counterproductive. International federations — UCI, British Cycling, USA Cycling, AusCycling — agree on one guiding principle: **skills before fitness, process before results, fun before competition**.

This document integrates scientific evidence, federation guidelines, and best practices from recognized coaches to provide a comprehensive framework guiding the preparation of the club's cyclists throughout a season with monthly regional competitions from February through October.

## 1. Physical and physiological development between ages 10 and 15 determines everything else

Understanding the biology of growth is the first requirement for training young athletes without causing harm. In this age range, three stages of maturation coexist — prepubertal, pubertal, and postpubertal — and individual variability is enormous: two 13-year-old cyclists can differ by up to 4 years of biological age.

### Growth and Peak Height Velocity (PHV)

The most relevant physiological milestone for the coach is the **Peak Height Velocity (PHV)**: the moment of maximum height growth rate. In girls it occurs on average at **11.5 years** (range 9–13), with rates of **7–9 cm/year**; in boys at **13.5 years** (range 11–15), reaching **8–10 cm/year**. Peak Weight Velocity (PWV) follows PHV by 6–14 months. During adolescence, approximately **50% of adult body weight** is acquired.

Before puberty, boys and girls develop at similar rates in speed, coordination, and strength. Strength gains in prepubertal youth are fundamentally **neural** (improved motor unit recruitment), not hypertrophic, due to hormonal immaturity. With puberty, testosterone increases up to 20 times in males, enabling significant muscle hypertrophy. Girls experience an increase in estrogens that modifies body composition and bone density. After PHV, boys show a marked "strength spurt" (12–18 months post-PHV), while in girls strength tends to stabilize.

### Windows of trainability according to the LTAD model

Balyi's LTAD model proposes sensitive periods for developing different physical capacities, summarized as follows:

| Quality | Girls | Boys |
|----------|-------|-------|
| **Speed (1st window)** | 6–8 years | 7–9 years |
| **Speed (2nd window)** | 11–13 years | 13–16 years |
| **Motor skill** | 8–11 years | 9–12 years |
| **Flexibility** | 6–10 years | 6–10 years |
| **Aerobic endurance** | At onset of PHV | At onset of PHV |
| **Strength (window 1)** | Immediately post-PHV | 12–18 months post-PHV |
| **Strength (window 2)** | At onset of menarche | — |

It is important to note that recent research (Lloyd and Oliver, 2012; YPD model) questions the rigidity of these "windows" and demonstrates that **all physical qualities are trainable at every stage of development**, although the mechanisms and magnitude of adaptation vary with maturation. The coach should use the windows as an orientative guide, not as an absolute prescription.

For the club's cyclists, this means that athletes aged **10–12** are mostly in the LTAD stage "Learn to Train" (training:competition ratio of **70:30**, participation in 3–4 sports), while those aged **13–15** are in the "Train to Train" stage (ratio **60:40**, prioritization of aerobic base, special emphasis on flexibility due to accelerated growth).

### Differences between boys and girls

The main differences relevant to planning are: girls reach PHV approximately two years earlier than boys, which advances the endurance and strength windows; girls maintain greater flexibility; after puberty, boys develop greater muscle mass and lower body fat percentage. **Before puberty, physical capacities are similar between both sexes**, and training can be practically identical. From ages 13–14 onward it is advisable to individualize, considering each athlete's biological maturation stage rather than chronological age.

### Risks of youth overtraining

The statistics are alarming: **70% of young athletes drop out of organized sport before age 13**, and 60% do so before age 15. The main causes are injury and burnout. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP, 2024) sets clear prevention guidelines that the club must adopt as non-negotiable rules: a single sport limited to **maximum 5 days/week**; at least **1 complete rest day per week**; **2–3 months of annual rest** from each individual sport; weekly training hours **should not exceed the athlete's chronological age** (a 12-year-old, maximum 12 hours); and no more than **8 months per year** training in a single sport.

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S), as defined by the IOC, is particularly relevant for cyclists given the lack of bone impact from cycling. Energy availability below **30 kcal/kg of fat-free mass/day** triggers metabolic and hormonal alterations, compromises bone mineral density (which peaks between ages 18–25), and can permanently affect bone health. Growth cartilages (physes) are biomechanically weaker than mature bone, with physeal injuries representing **15–30% of pediatric skeletal injuries**. Early specialization (before ages 12–14) multiplies injury risk by **1.5** and the risk of patellar tendinopathy and Osgood-Schlatter disease by **4**.

## 2. Physical capacities: what to develop and how to dose it by age

Elite XCO is raced at intensities of **~90% of maximum HR** and **~84% of VO₂max**, with more than 80% of race time above the lactate threshold, combining intermittent high-power efforts with active recoveries. Developing these capacities in young athletes requires a progressive approach adapted to maturation.

### Aerobic endurance

Children aged 8–13 show average improvements of only **5–6% in VO₂max** with endurance programs (compared to 15–20% in young adults). This does not mean aerobic training is useless, but that the predominant pathway for improvement in prepubertal youth is increased volume and natural maturation, not intensity. Before puberty, the bioenergetic profile is minimally modifiable by training; the lactic anaerobic pathway is immature. Children have smaller hearts, lower stroke volume, fewer red blood cells and hemoglobin. **Maximizing VO₂max should not be pursued in prepubertal cyclists.** After the onset of PHV, structured aerobic training becomes more meaningful.

**Practical load dosing:** at ages 10–11, predominance of fun rides, games, and relays on varied terrain (2–4 hours/week on the bike); at ages 12–13, gradual introduction of longer rides (60–90 min) and sustained-pace efforts (4–6 hours/week); at ages 14–15, structured base rides with tempo and threshold efforts (6–10 hours/week). The recommended intensity distribution is **~90% in low zones (Z1–Z2)** for early adolescents, evolving toward a polarized model (80% easy / 20% intense) for older athletes.

### Strength

The NSCA Position Statement (2009) establishes that there is no minimum age for strength training: readiness depends on emotional maturity and the ability to follow instructions. Supervised strength training **has no detrimental effect on linear growth** in children. For ages 10–11: bodyweight exercises (squats, push-ups, planks, medicine ball, jumps), 2 sessions/week of 20–30 minutes, focused on movement quality. For ages 12–13: add resistance bands and light dumbbells, supervised multi-joint exercises, 2–3 sessions/week of 30–40 minutes. For ages 14–15: progressive loading with free weights, periodized program, hypertrophy can begin (especially males 12–18 months post-PHV), cycling-specific exercises such as single-leg squat, deadlift, and step-ups, 2–3 sessions/week of 40–45 minutes. Load progression: **increase 5–10% when strength improves**, with 1–3 sets of 6–15 repetitions for general exercises and 1–3 sets of 3–6 repetitions for power exercises.

### Speed, coordination, and motor skills

The motor coordination window (8–11 years in girls, 9–12 in boys) is critical: a study with 203 children aged 6–10 found that **none mastered all 12 fundamental motor skills**. Children with high physical literacy are **twice as likely to remain active in sport**. Exercise-based injury prevention programs reduce youth injury rates by **46%**, and those including plyometrics by up to **55%**. Multi-sport participation reduces overuse injuries by up to **50%**.

For XCO specifically, the coach must develop agility, balance, and coordination through multi-surface and multi-sport activities (running, swimming, gymnastics, ball sports). Riding on varied terrain develops dynamic balance and proprioception. Cadence as a neuromuscular exercise (pedaling at high RPM in short durations) develops efficiency. Specific exercises include track stands, low-speed riding, tight turns, log crossings, and rock gardens.

| Capacity | 10–11 years | 12–13 years | 14–15 years |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Aerobic | Games, fun rides (2–4 h/week) | Long rides, steady state (4–6 h/week) | Base + structured tempo (6–10 h/week) |
| Strength | Bodyweight, medicine ball (2×/week, 20 min) | +Bands, light weights (2–3×/week, 30 min) | Progressive load, periodized (2–3×/week, 40 min) |
| Speed | Agility games, playful sprints (<5s) | Cadence exercises, standing starts | Race-specific sprints |
| Flexibility | General mobility, dynamic play | Cycling-specific mobility | Comprehensive flexibility program |
| Coordination | Multi-sport, varied surfaces | Specific skill sessions | Advanced handling, decision-making |

## 3. Technical progression in MTB/XCO: from basic position to bunny hop

Technical development in mountain biking follows a well-established hierarchy validated by organizations such as PMBIA, NICA, and BetterRide. The PMBIA **Six Fundamental Skills** pyramid builds from the base: position and balance → braking → bike-body separation → vision → cornering → terrain management. Each level depends on mastery of the previous one; an efficient turn cannot be executed without having mastered position, braking, bike-body separation, and vision.

### Fundamental skills and body position

The **neutral position** (standing on the pedals, knees and elbows slightly flexed, weight centered over the bottom bracket axle, eyes forward, pedals at 3 and 9 o'clock, heels slightly dropped) is the foundation of everything else. The **attack position** (elbows bent as if mid-push-up, hip hinged, knees bent, low center of gravity) is used for descents and technical terrain. The coach's verbal cue: **"heavy feet, light hands"** — weight through the pedals, relaxed grip on the handlebars.

Braking is the second skill to teach. The front brake provides **70–100% of braking power**. Controlled braking with both brakes is taught, avoiding wheel lock-up, braking *before* corners (never during), and using a one-finger technique for greater precision. The progression goes from low-speed braking → emergency stops → braking on varied terrain.

### Progression in cornering, climbs, and descents

Cornering is taught progressively: **flat corners** (wide entry, apex at the tightest point, wide exit; lean the bike, not the body; outside pedal down at 6 o'clock; look through the corner toward the exit) → **berms** (use the banked surface for support and speed generation) → **off-camber corners** (the most difficult; brake seriously before entry; chest over the handlebars; balanced weight) → **switchbacks** (slow entry, look through the entire turn, continuously adjusted weight distribution).

Climbs are taught in four stages (NICA methodology): dismount → seated → crouched → standing. For technical climbs over roots and rocks: maintain momentum, seated position for traction, smooth pedaling without wheel spin, look ahead. Descents follow a progression from wide paths with gentle slope → smooth singletrack → steeper singletrack with obstacles → technical descent with rocks, roots, and drops → high-speed descent with demanding line selection. Always with saddle lowered, attack position, weight back, light grip.

### Age-appropriate advanced skills

The **bunny hop** is fundamental for clearing trail obstacles and is taught with progression: front wheel lift → rear wheel lift → level lift → sequential bunny hop. Young riders have an advantage due to their lower center of gravity and smaller bikes. The **manual** (rolling with the front wheel raised without pedaling) requires only 1–2 seconds of duration to be useful on the trail. The **track stand** develops balance, control, and body awareness. The recommended progression is: wheelie → manual → wheel lifts → level lift → bunny hop.

### Teaching methodology

The **PMBIA IDEA methodology** structures each skill into four phases: **Introduce** (without obstacles) → **Develop** (repetition for muscle memory) → **Experiment** (in varied conditions) → **Apply** (on real terrain). NICA uses "Tell it, Show it, Do it, Review it". Lee McCormack, author of the NICA skills curriculum, emphasizes a deliberate sequential curriculum where each skill builds on the previous one, with strong emphasis on implicit and procedural practice rather than excessive conscious analysis.

**Pump tracks** are exceptional tools for youth development: they build cornering, body position, weight distribution, timing, balance, and coordination through repetition in a low-consequence environment. The pumping technique (generating speed through body movement) transfers directly to trail riding. **Skill parks** with progressive features (small drops, rollers, skinnies, rock gardens) allow practicing specific obstacles in a controlled setting.

The pre-ride safety check follows the **ABCDE protocol** (NICA): **A**ir (tire pressure), **B**rakes, **C**hain, **D**rivetrain, **E**verything else (quick releases, bolts, saddle height). Coaches must carry tools, first aid kit, nutrition, and communication devices at every practice.

## 4. Strategy and tactics in XCO racing

XCO competition has a highly stochastic physiological profile: average race power is approximately **76% of Critical Power (CP)**, with ~40% of time above CP and ~26% above Maximum Aerobic Power (MAP). Efforts above CP average ~8 seconds duration at ~120% of CP. Each athlete has a limited number of "matches" (intense efforts above threshold) that must be managed intelligently.

### Race phases and effort management

Research confirms a **"fast start" strategy**: the first lap is the fastest, with the magnitude of above-CP efforts averaging **11% of W' per effort** (compared to 3–5% in subsequent laps). The start is critically important because it determines position when entering the singletrack. After the first lap, the athlete transitions to a more uniform pacing pattern, with gradual power decrease per lap (positive pacing). Higher-level cyclists manage to accelerate in the final lap.

For teaching tactics to youth aged **10–12**, the focus should be limited to course familiarization, effort by feel, and fun while competing. For those aged **13–15**, concepts of lap timing, basic pacing, start technique, and line selection can be introduced. The most effective methods include: race simulations in training, course walks to discuss lines as a group, post-race analysis (what went well, what to improve), review of professional XCO video, and use of the cycling computer to develop pace awareness by comparing first and last lap splits.

### Pre-riding the course

Pre-riding is an essential competitive ritual: the first lap should be done at a slow and extremely observant pace (noticing technical sections, key climbs, narrow singletrack entries, passing zones, surfaces, gear selection). The second lap at moderate pace to practice chosen lines. At least parts of the course should be ridden at race pace, because technique differs significantly at competitive speed. Look for: corner radii that tighten or open, surface grip, gradient changes, dangerous obstacles, location of feed zones, and the transition from the start loop to the singletrack.

### Nutrition and hydration during races

For youth races (typically 20–60 minutes depending on category), in-race nutrition can be minimal. The focus should be on arriving well hydrated and fueled: carbohydrate-rich meal 3 hours before; light snack 1–2 hours before; optional gel 5–10 minutes before the start. In races longer than 45 minutes, water or sports drink can be taken at the feed zone. Practice drinking while pedaling during training. Dehydration of only **2% of body weight** affects decision-making, including line selection and distance judgment.

## 5. Periodization: how to structure a season with monthly competitions

With races approximately every 4 weeks from February through October (~9 competitions), the classic linear periodization model aimed at 1–2 annual peaks is inadequate. A **hybrid approach** is recommended: linear macro framework (gradual progression throughout the season) with undulating microcycles (weekly variation) and mini build-peak-race-recovery cycles within each 4-week mesocycle.

### Annual macrocycle structure

| Phase | Period | Duration | Main focus |
|------|---------|----------|-------------------|
| Transition/Rest | Nov (weeks 1–2) | 2–3 weeks | Active rest, free play, mental recovery |
| Preparation/Cross-training | Nov (week 3) – Dec | 4–6 weeks | Multi-sport, gym, skills, low intensity |
| Base 1 | January | 4 weeks | Aerobic foundation, basic strength, speed exercises |
| Base 2 | Feb (pre-1st race) | 3–4 weeks | Muscular endurance, tempo, volume increase |
| Build 1 / Competition onset | Mar – Apr | 6–8 weeks | Race-specific intensity; races every ~4 weeks |
| Build 2 / Mid-season | May – Jul | 10–12 weeks | Maintain fitness, peak for priority races |
| Peak / End of season | Aug – Sep | 6–8 weeks | Higher intensity, reduced volume, tapering |
| Final competition | Oct | 2–4 weeks | Final races, tapering, season close |
| Transition | Late Oct – Nov | 2–4 weeks | Active detraining, season review |

### Mesocycles and load management with monthly racing

Each 4-week mesocycle with a race at the end follows this structure: **Week 1** — moderate load (introduction of new stimulus); **Week 2** — increased load (+10–15% in volume or intensity, not both); **Week 3** — mini-tapering (reduce volume 30–40%, maintain 1–2 short intensity sessions); **Week 4** — race + recovery. It is essential to **classify races by priority**: 2–3 "A" races (full taper of 5–7 days), 3–4 "B" races (mini-taper of 3–4 days), and 2–3 "C" races (competed as part of training, no taper). C races serve as high-quality interval sessions without pressure for results.

### Typical weekly microcycle

**For ages 10–12 (3–5 hours/week, 3–4 sessions):** Monday rest or active play; Tuesday bike skills session (45–60 min); Wednesday rest or school sport; Thursday fun group ride with games and challenges (45–60 min); Friday rest; Saturday longer ride or trail outing (60–90 min); Sunday rest or family ride/other sport. At this age, **80% of training should be play-based**. Structured intervals are not necessary.

**For ages 13–15 (5–10 hours/week, 4–5 sessions):** Monday rest or light cross-training with mobility and core; Tuesday structured session with intervals (60–90 min, 1 key intensity session); Wednesday MTB technique/skills session (45–75 min); Thursday easy aerobic ride (60–75 min, Zone 1–2); Friday rest or strength training (30–45 min); Saturday long ride (90–150 min); Sunday rest or active recovery (30–45 min). Maximum **2 high-intensity sessions per week**. At least one weekly session should be purely recreational.

### Weekly training distribution (competitive phase, ages 13–15)

The recommended distribution is: Zone 1–2 endurance (**60–70%** of total time), technique/skills (**15–20%**), threshold/VO₂max intensity (**10–15%**, maximum 1–2 sessions/week), off-bike strength (1 session/week), speed/sprints (incorporated into 1 session/week with 10–30 second efforts).

### Off-season management (November–January)

The primary goal of the off-season is **mental recharge**. November: near-complete rest from cycling (maximum 1–2 very easy rides); encourage hiking, swimming, team sports; season review and achievement celebration. December: multi-sport participation (3–4 sessions/week of varied activities); start of functional/bodyweight strength (2×/week); 1–2 optional unstructured rides. January: gradual reintroduction of structured cycling (2–3 rides/week); all riding at low intensity (Z1–Z2); continue strength; start MTB skills work; sprint/speed exercises 1×/week (short, neuromuscular).

**Guiding principle:** moderate consistency outperforms seasonal overload. Four hours/week consistently throughout the year is superior to 10 hours/week for 3 months. Training should never compromise academic performance; during exam periods, reduce volume by 50% or more, maintaining 1–2 easy sessions as a stress-relief valve.

## 6. The young cyclist's mind: motivation, fear, and resilience

Self-Determination Theory (Ryan and Deci, 2000) identifies three basic psychological needs that drive intrinsic motivation: **autonomy** (feeling in control of decisions), **competence** (feeling capable and effective), and **relatedness** (connecting with others and a sense of belonging). A study with 492 soccer players aged 13–17 demonstrated that dropout is explained by greater amotivation and lower satisfaction of the needs for relatedness and autonomy. The coach at Club Trocha y Ruta must create a **mastery motivational climate** (where effort, personal improvement, and progress are valued) rather than an ego-oriented climate (where victories and comparisons are emphasized). The mastery climate is associated with lower anxiety, greater enjoyment, and reduced dropout.

### Managing fear on technical descents

Mountain biking generates specific fears from steep terrain, exposure, rocks, roots, and drops. The evidence-based strategies the coach must apply are: **progressive exposure** (start with slightly challenging features, gradually increase difficulty; each success builds confidence); **walking the line** (before riding difficult sections, walk them to review the trail, find the best route, and identify braking points); **visual demonstration** (an experienced rider demonstrates the feature so young riders can observe speed, line, gear selection, and body position); **visualization** (mental rehearsal of successful execution before the physical attempt); and **condition optimization** (attempt intimidating features when rested, in favorable weather and trail conditions, never at the end of training).

The verbal cue is: **"your bike follows your eyes"** — look where you want to go, not at the obstacle. Build skills separately from fear: practice techniques on easy terrain first, then apply on challenging terrain.

### Goals and communication

The goal-setting system for young athletes prioritizes three types in descending order of importance: **process goals** (focused on execution: "maintain attack position on every descent", "practice cornering 3 times this week"), **performance goals** (personal benchmarks: "complete the course 1 minute faster", "clean the rock section"), and **outcome goals** (external results: "finish in the top 10"), which should be used sparingly. 2–3 goals per training block (4–6 weeks) are recommended, with weekly reviews and written journals.

The coach's communication must maintain a **3:1 ratio of positive to corrective feedback**. Ask questions rather than give directives ("What happened in that corner?" vs. "You braked too late!"). Normalize errors as part of learning. Check how athletes feel, not just how they performed. Team cohesion is strengthened with mixed-skill group rides (mentorship pairs), team rituals (pre-race huddles, post-race debriefs), social activities outside of cycling, and a clear club identity (jersey, name, shared values).

Burnout prevention requires active monitoring of warning signs: chronic muscle soreness, decreased performance, loss of enthusiasm, increased irritability, fatigue that does not improve with rest, sleep and appetite changes, social withdrawal. Recovery from established burnout requires **4–12 weeks**. Parents should receive guidance at the start of the season on the development philosophy, with clear guides on what to say ("I love watching you ride") and what to avoid ("Why weren't you faster?"). The **"24-hour rule"** is recommended: do not analyze the race until one day later.

## 7. Nutrition for growing, training, and competing simultaneously

Young cyclists face a triple energy demand: growth, daily activities, and sports training. The target energy availability for young athletes is **≥45 kcal/kg of fat-free mass/day**. Basal caloric needs range from **1,600–2,200 kcal/day** (ages 9–13) and **2,000–2,600 kcal/day** (ages 14–15 depending on sex), to which **300–1,000+ kcal/day** must be added for training. The average for active young athletes is **2,500–3,500 kcal/day**.

The macronutrient distribution recommended by the Canadian Paediatric Society and the IOC is: **carbohydrates 45–65%** of total calories (6–10 g/kg/day depending on intensity; minimum RDA 130 g/day), being the most important fuel source since young athletes deplete glycogen faster than adults; **protein 15–20%** (~1.5 g/kg/day optimal, distributed as ~0.3 g/kg per meal, 5 meals/day at 3–4 hour intervals; intakes above 2.5 g/kg provide no additional benefit; 20 g of protein post-exercise maintains positive protein balance); **fats 25–35%** (critical for fat-soluble vitamins, essential fatty acids, and hormones).

### Nutritional timing

**Pre-training/competition:** complete meal 3–4 hours before (oatmeal + banana + peanut butter; pasta with light sauce); light snack 1–2 hours before (toast with jam, fruit, granola bar); small carbohydrate snack 30–60 minutes before if needed (banana, pretzel). Avoid high-fat and high-fiber foods close to exercise. **During exercise:** 150–300 mL of fluid every 15–20 minutes; for sessions under 1 hour, water is sufficient; for more than 60 minutes or in heat/humidity, sports drink with a carbohydrate concentration of **6%** and 20–30 mEq/L of sodium chloride. Small easily digestible carbohydrates every 30–45 minutes on long rides. **Post-exercise** (30–60 minute window): carbohydrates + protein in a **3:1 or 4:1** ratio; 0.3 g/kg of protein with high-glycemic-index carbohydrates. Chocolate milk is an excellent recovery option due to its natural ratio. Rehydrate with ~1.5 L of fluid per kg of body weight lost.

### Critical micronutrients and supplementation

**Calcium** is particularly important given that during the pubertal growth spurt ~300 mg/day accumulates; the recommendation is **1,300 mg/day** for ages 9–18 (sources: dairy, broccoli, spinach, fortified cereals). **Iron** is essential: 8 mg/day (ages 9–13), 11 mg/day (males 14–18), **15 mg/day** (females 14–18); the prevalence of iron deficiency reaches up to **51–68% in female athletes**; periodic screening is recommended for female cyclists and vegetarians. **Vitamin D** requires 600 IU/day; athletes at high latitudes or who train predominantly indoors are more susceptible to deficiency.

The consensus in the literature **does not support the use of supplements to improve performance in young athletes** (JPOSNA 2024; Canadian Paediatric Society). Protein supplements are unnecessary and may damage kidneys and cause dehydration in those under 18. Creatine has not been tested in those under 18 and is not recommended. Energy drinks (containing ~200 mg of caffeine per serving) cause hypertension, tachycardia, and sleep disorders, and should be strongly discouraged. The universal approach is **"food first"**; a multivitamin only if the diet is clearly insufficient, under medical supervision.

The coach must implement nutritional education strategies: nutrition workshops for athletes and parents, visual model of the "Performance Plate" (adapting portions for easy/moderate/intense training days), practical sessions on meal planning and snack preparation with the team.

## 8. Injury prevention, recovery, and growth as a risk factor

Overuse injuries represent approximately **50% of all youth sports injuries** (AAP). In mountain biking, the most common overuse injuries are knee pain from patellofemoral syndrome (the most frequent overuse area, related to saddle height, gear selection, and pedaling mechanics), lower back pain (**58%** of professional cyclists report it, due to the prolonged flexed position), and neck pain (up to **60%** of cyclists). Acute MTB-specific injuries include abrasions from falls, fractures (clavicle, wrist, and forearm are the most common), contusions, and sprains. Helmets reduce the risk of head injury by **85%**.

### Growth-related conditions

During growth spurts, bones lengthen before muscles and tendons fully adapt, creating relative inflexibility and increased tensile forces. **Osgood-Schlatter syndrome** (inflammation at the tibial tuberosity) is particularly prevalent in young cyclists during the growth spurt; it is treated with rest, ice every 2–3 hours, anti-inflammatories, and an infrapatellar strap. **Sever's disease** (calcaneal apophysitis) causes heel pain and is the most common heel complaint in children. **Sinding-Larsen-Johansson syndrome** affects where the patellar tendon inserts into the growth plate of the patella. All these conditions resolve when the growth plate closes, but require training load modification during the active episode.

### Warm-up and cool-down protocols

The pre-training dynamic warm-up (5–15 minutes) should include: general cardiovascular activation (3–5 min of easy spinning or light jog); dynamic stretches off the bike (leg swings front-back and lateral, 8–10 per leg; walking lunges with rotation, 8–10 per side; hip circles, 8–10 in each direction; high knees and heel-to-glute, 20 seconds each; bodyweight squats and glute bridges, 10 repetitions); and progressive warm-up on the bike (5–10 min of easy to moderate effort with 2–3 short 10–15 second accelerations). **Static stretching before exercise temporarily reduces power output** — use exclusively dynamic stretches pre-ride.

The post-training cool-down (10–15 min) includes easy spinning (5–10 min with progressive intensity decrease) followed by static stretches (hold 30–60 seconds each): hip flexor in kneeling lunge (**the most important stretch for cyclists**), hamstrings, quadriceps, calves, piriformis/gluteus, thoracic extension (with foam roller), pec/shoulder, and neck. Cycling generates predictable shortening of hip flexors, hamstrings, and the thoracic region.

### Sleep, recovery, and warning signs

Sleep requirements are **9–11 hours/night** for ages 6–12 (National Sleep Foundation) and **8–10 hours/night** for ages 13–18 (American Academy of Sleep Medicine), ideally with a 30-minute nap. Athletes who sleep fewer than 8 hours/night have a **1.7 times greater injury risk**. Growth hormone is secreted during deep sleep, which is critical for musculoskeletal development. Only **52% of competitive adolescent athletes** sleep 8+ hours.

Overtraining warning signs the coach must monitor include: persistent fatigue, recurring/chronic pain, decreased performance, frequent illness, loss of appetite, elevated resting heart rate, loss of enthusiasm, irritability, sleep disorders, social withdrawal, and academic decline. If resting HR is elevated by **>8 bpm for 3+ consecutive mornings**, impose an additional recovery day. During growth spurts: reduce repetitive volume, increase recovery, add cross-training, monitor pain complaints, and **adjust bike sizing every 3–6 months**.

Bike fit for growing cyclists is critical: saddle height with 25–30 degrees of knee flexion at the lowest pedal point, 5–8 cm of stand-over clearance between the top tube and the crotch for MTB, slight elbow bend with hands on grips, and bike weight ideally no more than **30–40% of the child's weight**. Size for current fit, not future growth, as an oversized bike undermines confidence and learning.

## 9. Technology in service of development, not the other way around

The cycling computers, heart rate monitors, and cadence sensors available at the club are valuable tools if used judiciously. Technology should support, not replace, the coach's intuition and the athlete's self-awareness.

### Heart rate in young athletes

The adult maximum HR formula (220 − age) **significantly overestimates** HRmax in children and is inaccurate for those under 18. Research by Gelbart et al. (2017, Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine) with 627 maximum tests on pediatric athletes (mean age 13.7 years) found a mean HRmax of **197 ± 8.6 bpm**, with age not being a significant predictor. The practical recommendation is to use **197 bpm as the standard estimate** for all children/adolescents, with **180 bpm as the minimum threshold** (−2 standard deviations).

Training zones by HR for young athletes (using 197 bpm as estimated HRmax) are: **Zone 1** (Recovery, 50–60%, 99–118 bpm); **Zone 2** (Endurance, 60–70%, 118–138 bpm); **Zone 3** (Tempo, 70–80%, 138–158 bpm); **Zone 4** (Threshold, 80–90%, 158–177 bpm); **Zone 5** (VO₂max, 90–100%, 177–197 bpm). For greater individualization, the **Karvonen method** (Heart Rate Reserve) can be used: Target HR = (HRR × desired intensity %) + resting HR. Example: a 13-year-old cyclist with resting HR of 65 bpm would have Zone 2 between 144–157 bpm. For ages 10–12, formal HRmax testing is not recommended; use the 197 bpm estimate or simply rely on RPE. For ages 13+, with supervision, a field test can be conducted (2–3 minutes of climbing with progressive effort to maximum).

### Cadence and relevant metrics

Target cadence ranges are: **70–85 rpm** for ages 10–12 (low gears, focus on smooth pedaling, avoid "stomping"), **75–90 rpm** for ages 13–15 (gradually building toward adult ranges). **Low-cadence work (50–60 rpm) is not recommended** for those under 15 due to excessive stress on developing joints and tendons. Cadence exercises should be playful: "spin-up challenges" (30–60 second intervals at progressively higher cadences with quiet torso), "cadence match" (riding at the cadence indicated by the coach, switching between 70, 80, and 90 rpm), and "gear exploration" (riding the same segment in 3 different gears observing how cadence and effort change).

The primary metric for managing youth training volume is **time/duration**, not distance (which is too variable due to terrain and conditions). HR is important for monitoring aerobic rides and recovery trends. Cadence is moderately useful for technical development. Speed has **low priority** (too variable; can create unhealthy competition). Elevation gain is useful for quantifying climbing load in MTB. Metrics to avoid with young athletes include TSS, IF, normalized power (too complex), and Strava segments/KOMs (encourage dangerous riding).

### RPE as the primary tool

The **Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE)** should be the primary intensity monitoring tool for youth cyclists, backed by HR as a secondary metric. The OMNI scale (0–10), validated for children aged 8+, shows correlations of **0.85–0.94** with HR and VO₂ in children aged 9+, and **0.94–0.99** in ages 12–15. Simplified language for prescription is: "easy conversation pace" (Z1–2), "I can say short sentences" (Z3), "I can only say 1–2 words" (Z4), "I can't speak" (Z5). Ask cyclists to rate their RPE after each session and record it in a training journal.

**Power meters** are not recommended for ages 10–12 (too young for data-driven training; focus on fun, skills, RPE); are optional/not necessary for ages 13–14 (HR + RPE are sufficient); and can be beneficial from age 15 onward for pacing, race analysis, and structured training. If a roller-based power meter is available, use the data for coach analysis without the cyclist training "by numbers". At least one weekly ride should be done **with screens off** — ride by feel.

## 10. What the federations say: regulations and youth development philosophy

The UCI does not directly govern cyclists under 15 years at the international level; categories below U17 are the responsibility of **national federations**. The youngest UCI category for international XCO is Junior (U19, 17–18 years), with a target race duration of approximately **1 hour to 1:15**. XCC (Short Track) only admits athletes 19 and older; XCE (Eliminator) from age 17. There are no UCI gear restrictions for junior MTB (unlike track and road). Wheels must have a diameter between 55–74 cm including tire.

### Categories and race durations by national federation

The main federations establish categories and duration limits that provide a clear reference framework:

- **AusCycling:** U11 (9–10 years, 15–25 min), U13 (11–12, 15–30 min), **U15 (13–14, 40–45 min)**, U17 (15–16, 50 min), U19 (17–18, 50–60 min). Their philosophy: *"In younger categories the emphasis should be entirely on fun, skill development, leg speed and learning. Endurance naturally develops as physical maturity progresses."*
- **Cycling Canada:** U13 (15–20 min), U15 (25–30 min), U17 (35–40 min), U19 (60+ min). U15 and younger compete in skill-based categories (Novice, Sport, Expert).
- **British Cycling:** Youth C (10–11), Youth B (12–13), Youth A (14–15), Junior (16–18). The **Go-Ride** program is the introductory program for those under 16, based on the **"stage, not age"** principle with four badges: Balance → Pedal → Rider Challenge → Advanced Rider. More than **60% of British national team cyclists** started in Go-Ride clubs.
- **USA Cycling:** Junior categories 9–10, 11–12, 13–14, 15–16. Their philosophy: *"Rushing into high-mileage or highly structured training is generally unnecessary and counterproductive for younger junior cyclists."* NICA (interscholastic competition) operates in 32 state leagues with a **"No try-outs. No bench. Everyone rides."** policy.
- **Colombia:** Pre-infantil (10–11), Infantil (13–14), Pre-juvenil (15–16), Juvenil (17–18). La Vuelta del Porvenir and La Vuelta del Futuro are the key national competitions. Club Esteban Chaves works on youth development from age 6 with a methodology centered on "fun and values formation".
- **Chile:** Promocional 2 (9–10, from 3 km), Promocional 3 (11–12, from 6 km), Juvenil (13–14, from 12 km), Cadetes (15–17). Minimum registration age with the federation: 5 years.
- **Argentina (FACIMO):** Has "Escuelas de Formación" and "Centros de Desarrollo Deportivo de MTB". For formative and school events, the philosophy is explicit: *"There should be no winners or losers. Everyone receives a medal or diploma."*

### Universal principles from the federations

All major federations converge on the same fundamental principles: **fun first** (enjoyment is the primary driver at early ages); **skills before fitness** (technical development is prioritized over endurance and power for ages 10–15); **multi-sport participation** (specialization is discouraged before ages 15–16; USA Cycling articulates it: *"Encourage them to participate in other sports throughout the year. Variety is good for athletic, cognitive, and socioemotional development"*); **progressive load** (volume and intensity must increase gradually with biological maturation); **process over results** (growth and development over race rankings); **safeguarding** (background checks, child protection training, and qualified coaching mandatory at all major federations).

WADA provides youth-specific anti-doping educational materials: a Youth course (ages 11–15) on the ADEL platform, available in Spanish, focused on instilling fair-play values before exposure to prohibited substances.

## Conclusions: an athlete-centered training system, not a results-centered one

Training XCO cyclists aged 10–15 is not a scaled-down version of adult training. It is an integral construction process where mountain biking is the vehicle for developing more complete, athletic, and resilient human beings. The data is unequivocal: early specialization increases injuries, accelerates burnout, and does not predict elite success (the success rate of highly ranked juniors reaching elite level is below 20%).

The coach at Club Trocha y Ruta must adopt three non-negotiable commitments: **first**, respect the biology of growth by adapting loads to individual maturation (not chronological) and reducing volume during growth spurts; **second**, maintain fun as the leading indicator of program success, knowing that 70% of youth dropout is prevented by satisfying autonomy, competence, and relatedness; and **third**, use available technology (cycling computers, HR, cadence) as a support tool subordinate to RPE and direct observation, never as a dictator of training.

With a 9-month season and monthly races, the optimal periodization is a hybrid macrocycle with 4-week mesocycles integrating mini build-taper-race-recovery cycles, classifying competitions by priority (A/B/C) to avoid the wear of continuously tapering. The technical development pyramid — position, braking, bike-body separation, vision, cornering, terrain — must be worked with the same systematicity as physical fitness, because in XCO **technical skill saves energy that fitness cannot compensate for**. Pump tracks and skill parks are high-return investments for the club. Nutrition requires active education of families, prioritizing adequate energy availability for growth + sport + daily life, with an inflexible "food first, zero supplements" approach.

The result of applying this framework will not be measured in immediate podiums, but in athletes who reach ages 17–18 with a solid aerobic base, refined technique, a healthy relationship with competition, and — most importantly — still wanting to keep pedaling.
